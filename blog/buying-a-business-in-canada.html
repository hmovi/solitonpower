Buying a businessNovember 28, 20267 min read

Looking to buy a business in Canada? Here’s what to do, and how we help.

By the team at Soliton Power

Starting a company from scratch in a new country takes time. Buying one that already has customers, staff, suppliers and a track record can get you there years faster. Many Canadian business owners are looking for the right buyer: someone who will keep their business running and growing.

Here’s how to do it properly, whether you live in Canada or are investing from abroad.

What you need to do

1. Know what you’re looking for

Decide on the industry, size, location and role you want. Will you run it yourself, or put a manager in place? A clear brief saves months of looking at the wrong businesses.

2. Get your finances ready

Know your budget, how much you can put down, and how you’ll fund the rest. That might mean your own capital, a bank loan, investors, or seller financing, where the current owner is paid part of the price over time.

3. Understand the rules for foreign buyers

If you’re not Canadian, the federal Investment Canada Act applies when you acquire control of a Canadian business. For most small and medium-sized deals, this means filing a short notification with the government, which can be done before closing or within 30 days after. A full “net benefit” review only applies to much larger deals, worth well over a billion dollars in 2026.

There are exceptions. The government can review any foreign investment on national security grounds, and some sensitive sectors face extra requirements, while cultural businesses such as media and publishing have their own rules. If you also want to live and work in Canada, immigration is a separate process with its own requirements, so speak to a licensed immigration professional early.

4. Value the business

The asking price is the seller’s opinion. You need your own view of what the business is worth, based on its real earnings, assets, customers and risks. Small businesses are often valued as a multiple of the owner’s earnings, but every business is different.

5. Do your due diligence

Before you commit, check everything: financial statements and tax filings, contracts and leases, staff and payroll, licences and permits, customers and suppliers, and any debts or legal issues. What you find here often changes the price or the deal terms.

6. Choose the deal structure

You can buy the company’s shares, which brings its history and liabilities with it, or buy its assets, such as equipment, inventory, customer lists and the brand, and leave the old company behind. The right choice depends on taxes, risk and the business itself, so take professional advice.

7. Plan the handover

The first 100 days matter. Keep the staff, keep the customers, keep the suppliers. A good transition plan, often with the seller staying on for a period, protects what you paid for.

Nigerian companies: it’s time to look at Canada and the US

By the team at Soliton Power

Nigeria has some of the most ambitious founders and exporters in the world. Yet for most Nigerian companies, North America is still a market they talk about rather than one they sell into. That’s starting to change, and the timing has rarely been better.

The numbers are moving in your favour

Trade between the two countries has grown quietly but steadily. Nigeria became Canada’s second-largest trading partner in Africa in 2025, with about $3.2 billion in two-way trade, according to the Government of Canada. The two governments have also been talking about a Foreign Investment Promotion and Protection Agreement, designed to give investors on both sides more certainty.

Canada is actively looking beyond its biggest neighbour. Ottawa has set a goal of doubling exports to markets outside the United States by 2035, and opened a Strategic Exports Office this summer to help get there. More trade with partners like Nigeria is part of that plan, and that creates openings for Nigerian companies on the import side too.

South of the border, the US has renewed the African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA). It gives eligible African countries, including Nigeria, duty-free access to the US for more than 1,800 products. After lapsing in September 2025 and being restored for a single year in February, AGOA was extended again this month, this time to the end of 2028. That gives exporters a longer window to plan, sign contracts and invest.

Where the opportunity is

For years, most of Nigeria’s exports to the US have been crude oil and energy products. The bigger opportunity now is everything else: the products and services Nigerian companies already sell well at home and across Africa.

  • Food and agri-products. Processed foods, spices, cocoa products, shea and specialty ingredients, sold to the large diaspora and mainstream grocery markets in both countries.
  • Fashion, beauty and consumer goods. Nigerian design and brands already have global recognition. North American retail and e-commerce can turn that into steady revenue.
  • Technology and fintech. Nigeria’s tech companies have built products for demanding, fast-moving markets. Canada and the US offer partners, capital and customers.
  • Creative and professional services. Media, education, software and outsourced services can be sold across borders with relatively low set-up costs.

What usually goes wrong

Most companies don’t fail in North America because their product isn’t good enough. They stumble on the details:

  • Product labelling, food safety or import rules they didn’t know applied to them.
  • No local buyer, distributor or partner lined up before the first shipment.
  • Landed costs (freight, duties, warehousing and fees) that wipe out the margin.
  • Marketing that works in Lagos but doesn’t land with a buyer in Toronto or Houston.
  • Trying to do everything at once, in two countries, without a clear first step.

The bottom line

The door between Nigeria and North America is more open than it has been in years. The companies that benefit will be the ones that prepare properly, starting now rather than waiting for the perfect moment.
